Section 01

Introduction & Motivation

Why receptive fields and frequency response determine whether a deep learning model succeeds or fails in remote sensing — and why the right architecture choice is non-trivial.

What Are Receptive Fields?

The receptive field of a neuron in a deep network is the region of the input image that can influence its activation. For a single convolutional neuron three layers deep (kernel=3, stride=1), the theoretical RF is $\text{RF} = 1 + 3 \times (3-1) = 7$ pixels. This is a tiny window into a scene.

But receptive fields come in two flavours that are very different in practice:

Theoretical RF

The maximum region geometrically reachable by backpropagation through the network topology. Calculated from kernel sizes and strides. Formula: $\text{RF}_L = 1 + \sum_{i=1}^{L}(k_i-1)\prod_{j<i}s_j$

Effective RF (ERF)

The region that actually influences the output, measured by input gradients. Luo et al. (2017) showed that ERF follows a Gaussian distribution — pixels far from the centre contribute negligibly despite being in the theoretical RF.

Remote Sensing Challenges

Remote sensing images are fundamentally different from natural images:

Multi-Scale Objects

A single aerial image may contain buildings (10–50 m), roads (5–20 m wide), forests (hectares to km²), and entire river systems — spanning 3–4 orders of magnitude in spatial scale. A model whose RF is fixed at 9 pixels cannot reason about a 500-pixel lake.

Spectral Diversity

Remote sensing sensors capture beyond the visible spectrum: near-infrared (NIR), shortwave infrared (SWIR), synthetic aperture radar (SAR), and hyperspectral (100+ bands). The frequency content and spatial statistics differ drastically between modalities:

  • SAR: dominated by speckle noise (high spatial frequencies)
  • Multispectral: smooth gradients, sharp boundaries (mixed frequencies)
  • Hyperspectral: highly correlated spectral bands, structured spectral sequences

Large Image Tiles

Remote sensing images are typically 512×512 to 4096×4096 pixels per tile. A CNN with $\text{RF}=9$ on a 512-pixel image sees only $(9/512)^2 = 0.03\%$ of the scene per neuron — catastrophic for land-use classification that requires global context.

⚠ Scale Mismatch Problem
A 4-layer CNN with kernel=3, stride=1 has theoretical RF = 9 px. On a 512×512 remote sensing image, this is 1.76% of the scene width. No amount of training will compensate for a blind spot this large.

About This Analysis

We implement three 4-layer models in PyTorch from scratch — SmallCNN, SmallViT, SmallMamba — trained on a toy 10-class dataset and analysed with two complementary methods:

  1. Gradient-based ERF: Backpropagate from centre pixel → |grad| map
  2. Fourier frequency analysis: FFT of features for sinusoidal grating inputs

All models run on CPU without any GPU requirement, completing all experiments in under 10 minutes.
